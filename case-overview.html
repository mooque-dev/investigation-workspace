01

The reframe

Stripped of domain detail, this is one problem: when a busy person is told something is wrong, how do they use an AI partner to find the cause, decide what to do, and leave a defensible record? The bioreactor is context. The real design tension is that the AI must be fast and autonomous enough to save an interrupted operator real time, while staying restrained and transparent enough to be safe in a setting where a confident wrong answer can scrap a multi-million-dollar batch. Everything below is the resolution of that tension.

02

The core idea: an autonomy ladder

One reusable rule governs the product's behavior — match the level of human control to the cost and reversibility of the action. Not "AI does everything" or "AI asks for everything," but four calibrated rungs.

Fully autonomousFull human control
RUNG 1
Does it silently
Read-only, reversible work — pull data, validate the signal, scan recent events. No gate. It just happens the instant you arrive.
RUNG 2
Shows its work
Forms ranked hypotheses with evidence for, evidence against, and confidence. Never one confident answer.
RUNG 3
You sign off
Drafts the record, the escalation, or a heavier analysis. AI prepares; you commit.
RUNG 4
You act, not it
Recommends an equipment change you make yourself. The AI has no write access to the reactor, by design.
This ladder generalizes past pH — it's a pattern for any AI that assists with consequential decisions in a regulated setting.

04

Designing for an AI that's wrong sometimes

Communicating uncertainty. Never one answer — a ranked field with confidence and, crucially, evidence against each hypothesis. Showing what argues against the leading theory is what lets a domain expert overrule it in seconds. The AI also states what it checked and what it couldn't.

Recovering when it's wrong — the part most concepts skip. The human can reject the top cause in one tap (logged as signal). After a recommended action, the system watches for non-recovery and auto-reopens. A persistent escalate path routes to a human expert with the full case file attached, so escalation costs seconds, not a re-explanation.

And the record separates what the AI suggested from what you decided from what actually worked — so a wrong suggestion you correctly overrode reads as a system success, not a failure.

05

How we'd know it works

North-star metric
Time from notification to a documented, correct mitigation decision
Speed and quality together. "Correct" is validated against the logged outcome at batch close — optimizing speed alone would be easy and wrong.
Hypothesis hit-rate
Was the acted-on cause actually right, confirmed at outcome?
Override + recovery rate
A healthy system is overruled sometimes — and recovers well when it is.
Gate friction
Are checkpoints landing where cost is, or are people rubber-stamping them?
Record completeness
Is the audit trail whole at close, with no manual backfill?
Trust calibration as a metric, not a vibe: the gap between user-stated confidence and the AI's actual accuracy. Over-trust and under-trust are both failures — and both are designable.
→ week one: three risks I'd kill first
R1Do they trust the rule-out-the-boring-stuff-first order, or do experts want to jump straight to a cause? Sit with 3–4 floor managers through replayed excursions.
R2Are the gate moments landing where the cost actually is, or are they friction that trains people to click through?
R3Does feedback-as-byproduct survive real time pressure, or do people blow past it the moment they're rushed?
06

What I deliberately didn't solve

Naming the boundary is a design decision, not an omission.

✕
The AI never writes to equipment. It recommends; you execute. A safety and regulatory boundary, on purpose.
✕
No alarming or detection. The plant system owns that; we begin at "already notified."
✕
No autonomous escalation or external notification without explicit human sign-off.
✕
No fleet view, shift handoff, or analytics. The unit of design here is a single investigation.
07

Does it generalize?

The real test of this design isn't the pH screen — it's whether the same machinery absorbs a different alarm. It does. What changes is the differential (the domain knowledge that lives in rung 2). What stays is the entire spine.

Invariant across all three
The product is the pattern, not the pH screen.
  • The autonomy ladder — same four rungs
  • The flow shape — orient → rule out → hypothesize → decide → document → close
  • Where the gates fall — at the same cost / irreversibility points
  • The failure loop — non-recovery reopens and re-ranks
  • Feedback as a byproduct — confirm / reject is the label
  • One record — the audit trail is the training corpus
This is what makes it a platform pattern, not a feature: a new alarm type plugs in by swapping the differential — not redesigning the experience.
