The Investigation Workspace

Founding / Staff Product Designer challenge. The product argument in depth — start from the case overview for the one-screen version.

0.The one-line thesis

A fast junior analyst that does the legwork and shows its work — so a time-pressured floor manager reaches a trustworthy, documented decision, and is never handed a confident wrong answer.

1.The reframe

Stripped of domain detail, the brief is one problem: when a busy person is told something is wrong, how do they use an AI partner to find the cause, decide what to do, and leave a defensible record? The bioreactor is the context that makes the problem hard, not the problem itself.

The central tension is a pull in two directions at once. The AI has to be fast and autonomous enough to genuinely save an interrupted operator time — landing them on a worked-up case instead of a blank prompt. And it has to be restrained and transparent enough to be safe in a GxP environment, where a confident wrong answer can scrap a multi-million-dollar batch or trigger a formal deviation. Everything below is a consequence of holding both at once.

The brief says twice that this is "not a chat box" and should be "more agentic than simple Q&A." I read that literally: the unit of work is not a message, it's an investigation with state — a plan whose steps have status, where the AI executes the cheap and safe ones itself and gates at the consequential ones.

2.Assumptions

  1. The alert already exists. Something upstream flagged BR-04 as out of band. I'm designing the investigation, not the detector.
  2. The operator knows this reactor better than the model. The human is the senior party. The AI is the fast analyst, not the oracle.
  3. GxP applies. Every consequential action is documented: who, what, when, why. This is non-negotiable and shapes the whole surface.
  4. Actions are enacted in a separate validated control system. The workspace can draft a corrective action; a human enacts it elsewhere. The AI never writes to the PLC/SCADA.
  5. Primary surface is desktop (control-room / office), with a tablet companion for the floor. Not mobile-first.

If any of these are wrong — especially #1 or #4 — the design changes materially, which is why they lead.

3.Scope — and what I cut on purpose

Restraint is the senior signal. The brief lists a large menu of what the AI can do; using all of it would be the junior move.

In scope

  • Investigating a single flagged excursion end to end
  • Autonomous evidence gathering and correlation across historical batches and logs
  • A ranked differential with confidence and evidence for and against
  • Drafting a recommended corrective action (gated on human approval)
  • Capturing the investigation as one artifact that serves as both audit record and training label

Cut on purpose

  • Writing to the control system — safety and regulatory non-starter
  • The alerting/detection system — assumed upstream
  • Predictive / preventive alerting — a compelling v2, out of this scenario
  • Multi-batch fleet dashboards — different job, different surface
  • Most of the capability menu — used only what this investigation needs, and said so

4.The autonomy ladder

Autonomy is the core design variable, and it's not a switch — it's a ladder. The design decision is where the gate sits.

RungStepAutonomyWhy there
0Observe — read sensor trace, pull batch recordFully autonomous, no gateRead-only, zero batch risk
1Correlate — comparable batches, CIP & maintenance logsAutonomous, loggedStill read-only; this is the legwork
2Hypothesize — rank causes with confidence + evidenceAutonomous, presented for reviewAnalysis, not action
3Recommend an actionGated — human approves with a recorded rationaleFirst step that could touch the batch
4Execute on the control systemOut of scopeAI never enacts; human does, elsewhere

The operator returns to a case already worked up through rung 2. The AI's hand never reaches rung 4.

5.The flow

  1. Alert lands. A labeled out-of-band card — icon, "OUT OF BAND," reactor, value, target. The investigation auto-starts.
  2. AI climbs rungs 0–2 autonomously, logging each step. By the time the operator looks, there's a ranked differential.
  3. Operator reviews the differential. Each hypothesis expands to confidence + evidence for and against. They can agree, dig in, or redirect.
  4. Operator requests a recommendation. The AI drafts a reversible, in-range corrective action at rung 3 — and stops.
  5. The gate. Approving requires typing why. No one-click accept. Reject sends it back for alternatives.
  6. Human enacts the action in the validated control system (rung 4, outside this tool).
  7. Case closes into a single file that is both the deviation record and a labeled training example.

The three directions in the mock (A · Differential, B · The gate, C · Closed file) are the three load-bearing moments of this flow.

6.The failure we design against

The dangerous failure mode is not "the AI is wrong." Models are wrong sometimes; that's survivable when the human catches it. The dangerous mode is "the AI is confidently wrong and the human defers." Three concrete risks and their mitigations:

  • Overconfidence. Mitigation: never a single answer. A ranked differential, every hypothesis paired with evidence against it and a confidence value. A verdict can't masquerade as a fact.
  • Automation complacency. Mitigation: the gate can't be rubber-stamped. Approving requires a recorded rationale — the human has to articulate why, which is also the deviation record.
  • Alert fatigue / attention misspend. Mitigation: the AI earns autonomy on cheap steps so human attention is reserved for the gate, where it actually matters.

7.The artifact — one thing, two jobs

The strongest single idea here: the investigation case file — every AI step, every piece of evidence, every human decision and its rationale — is simultaneously the GxP compliance record and the labeled-data corpus.
  • As a record (top-to-bottom): trigger, evidence reviewed, hypotheses, the human decision and rationale, who enacted it, the outcome. Audit-ready, no manual rework.
  • As a label (as fields): { inputs, ranked_hypotheses, accepted, rejected, rationale, outcome } — exactly the supervision the next model needs, including why a human accepted or overrode the top hypothesis.

Designing these as one artifact means the documentation a regulator demands is the documentation that improves the model. The compliance burden becomes the data flywheel.

8.How we'd know it works

  • Time to trustworthy decision (primary) — alert acknowledged → documented decision. Compress this without cutting corners.
  • Top-hypothesis override rate — a calibration signal. We want it neither at zero (blind trust) nor high (a useless model); a healthy band shows the human is genuinely checking.
  • Deviation-record completeness — every closed case produces an audit-ready record with no rework. Target 100%.
  • Label yield — share of investigations producing usable accepted/rejected labels with rationale.
  • Guardrail: false-alarm precision on the upstream alert, watched so the workspace isn't training operators to ignore it.

9.Validation plan

  1. Shadow mode. The AI investigates in parallel with no operator-facing UI. Compare its differentials to human conclusions on closed cases. Cheap, zero risk, calibrates confidence before anyone trusts it.
  2. Advisory mode, small cohort. A few experienced operators see the workspace but every action still goes through the gate. Watch time-to-decision and override rate.
  3. Widen only once override rate sits in a healthy band and record completeness holds — never by giving the AI more autonomy past rung 3.

10.Open questions I'd take into the interview

  • Where exactly is the rung-3/rung-4 boundary in your validated systems — is "draft an action" already too much in some contexts?
  • Who owns a case when shifts change mid-investigation? The case file makes handoff legible, but the ownership model needs your input.
  • How much of the historical-batch corpus is actually queryable today? The correlate rung assumes more than it might have.
